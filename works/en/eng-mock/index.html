2. A Reading: Glamping Gone Wrong

[1] When Jasmine Lau, a 15-year-old from Hong Kong, saw the photos of “Starlight Glamping” online, she thought she had found the perfect weekend getaway for her family. The website promised spacious tents with real beds, a private bathroom for every tent and a breathtaking view of the lake. “It looked like a five-star hotel in the middle of nature,” she said. The family booked two nights for $3,600 and waited eagerly for their trip.

[2] Her excitement did not last long. When the family arrived, Jasmine was horrified. Water was dripping from a hole in the roof of the tent, and the mattress was covered in mould. Instead of a private bathroom, there was only one toilet, which was 500 metres away and shared by about 30 campers. “To make things worse, the ‘lake view’ was actually a muddy pond,” Jasmine said.

[3] The first night was a nightmare. A heavy rainstorm hit the campsite and water flowed into the tent. Jasmine woke up at 3 a.m. to find her sleeping bag completely soaked. The tents were also so close together that the family could hear every word their neighbours said. “We felt as if we were sleeping in a public park,” said her mother. The next morning, the family reluctantly decided to leave a day early.

[4] Jasmine’s mother called the owner many times, but nobody answered. A week later, she finally received an email offering a refund of only $500. “It was nowhere near enough to make up for our ruined weekend,” she said. She has since written a detailed review online to warn other families.

[5] Travel experts say that stories like Jasmine’s are becoming more common as glamping grows in popularity. They advise travellers to read recent reviews from other guests instead of relying on the photos on a website, and to check the cancellation policy before paying. “Photos can be edited, but hundreds of honest reviews cannot,” said travel blogger Sam Wong.

[6] Despite her experience, Jasmine has not given up on glamping. “When it is done well, glamping lets you sleep under the stars and wake up to birdsong without giving up comfort,” she said. “Next time, I will just do my homework first.”

6. H Bonus: Game On — or Game Over?

Online gaming has become one of the most common hobbies among teenagers. In a survey of secondary students, seven in ten said they play online games every day, and one in five plays for more than three hours a day.

Gaming is not all bad. Some games improve problem-solving skills and teamwork, and many teenagers say gaming helps them relax after school. However, experts warn that playing for long hours can lead to poor eyesight, back pain and falling grades. Many games also use reward systems, such as daily login prizes and loot boxes, which make it hard for players to stop.

Experts suggest that teenagers set a time limit and take a ten-minute break every hour. Parents are encouraged to play together with their children, as this helps them understand the games and builds closer family bonds.

2 What makes it hard for players to stop? → reward systems such as daily login prizes and loot boxes

3 How can parents benefit from playing with their children? → it helps them understand the games / builds closer family bonds
